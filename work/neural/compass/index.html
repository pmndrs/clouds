HaloRT runs on our own hand-written WGSL engine and matches the authors' output. The live lab page still uses ONNX Runtime; wiring the engine into it is the next task.

A wrong guess, caught earlyI blamed ONNX Runtime's 1,825 dispatches per frame for the slow network. Measured, a dispatch costs about 6.5 µs, so that explains about 12 ms. The real gap is kernel speed.

What the research turned up

It already runs in a browserThe authors publish a WebGPU demo: features in WGSL, the network in ONNX Runtime Web. On this Mac it takes 47–55 ms per frame at 256² and 170–210 ms at 512².
Weights yes, training noModel code, weights and the feature shader are MIT. Training code, the 22 000 training images and the environment maps are not public.
The code is not the paperFive feature encodings and the attention block differ from what the paper prints. The weights were trained on the code, so the port follows the code.
Time of day needs no trainingSun and sky are inputs. A different hour is a different sun direction and a different baked sky, fed to the same weights.
We own most of the partsVDB to 3D texture, the sky, a validated path tracer for ground truth, and a WGSL U-Net engine that runs a similar-sized network in about 5 ms per pass.
Nobody has shipped thisWe found no game, engine or browser product using a neural cloud renderer. The authors' demo is the only one on the web.

How HaloRT works

1 · MarchOne primary ray per pixel through the density grid. Record optical depth and four depths where scaled versions of the medium would first scatter.5 channels
2 · Look at the lightFrom the first of those depths: one shadow ray to the sun, about eight shadowed rays to the sky.1 + 3 channels
3 · InferA 1.2 M-parameter U-Net with local attention turns eight feature images into one radiance image. It runs six times: sun and sky, three colours.8 in → 1 out, ×6
4 · CompositeScale by sun and sky intensity, multiply by the pixel's opacity, add the background.no network

It never traces a scattering path and never sees a radiance sample. Because the features are nearly noise-free, the output is stable from frame to frame without any temporal model.

Limits the authors state: clouds only, Henyey–Greenstein phase only, one volume, distant light only, no surfaces or lights inside the medium, synthetic training environments.

Where neural cloud rendering stands

Five families, told apart by where the network sits.

FamilyNetwork sitsExamplesTrainedSpeed, their hardwareNew clouds
1 · Raymarch + analytic scatteringNoneNubis, Frostbite, our library—A few msYes
2 · Radiance prediction per pointInside the marchDeep Scattering, MRPNN, WebVDB's 'neural' modeOnceMinutes (2017) → 25–33 ms (2023)Yes
3 · Baked per cloudGrid + tiny MLP per assetRNA, Olajos 2026, MRBNNPer cloud, 20 min – 4 h0.4–5 msNo, rebake
4 · Denoise a few path samplesImage space, after path tracingHofmann 2020–23, Xu 2025Once24–60 ms, grows with densityYes
5 · Infer from primary-ray featuresImage space, no pathsHaloRTOnce10 ms at 512²Yes

Settled

  • Multiple scattering in clouds is smooth enough that a small network gets it right from cheap inputs.
  • The useful inputs are always the same few: optical depth, transmittance toward the light at several scales, phase and albedo.
  • Sun and sky are inferred separately and scaled outside the network.

Open

  • No method has a temporal model. Moving clouds and cameras are untested.
  • All of them light one cloud in a box. None handles a cloudscape, aerial perspective or terrain inside the cloud.
  • Training is not reproducible from public code for HaloRT, MRPNN or MRBNN.
  • Everything uses Henyey–Greenstein. Dual-lobe and Mie phases have no counterpart.

Does HaloRT build on the papers it cites?

It builds on three: Hofmann's volume denoisers (their feature-image idea and training settings, minus the noisy radiance input), Deep Shading (shade from feature buffers alone), and HaloNet (local attention). It compares against MRPNN and OIDN, takes its clouds from the same group's Cloudy dataset, and cites the remaining forty or so as background.

How it meets a raymarched renderer

Both do the same first half: march the primary ray, march toward the sun, read the sky. They differ in what they do with the numbers.

StepOur raymarcherHaloRT
Primary marchPer step: density, accumulate colourOnce: optical depth and four crossing depths
Sun shadowLight march or light grid at every stepOne shadow ray at one point per pixel
SkyEight hemisphere means, unshadowedAbout eight shadowed, importance-sampled rays
Multiple scatteringOctave or fit formula per stepA U-Net over the feature images
CostSteps × light tapsFeatures (cheap) + network (fixed)
DetailEvery step shades, so edges are as sharp as the densityFine detail has to come through the features

Three ways to combine them

  1. Network as the lighting model for a hero cloud. Keep our density, VDB loading, sky and compositing; replace one cloud's lighting with the feature pass and network. This is the plan's main line.
  2. Network for multiple scattering only. Keep our marched single scattering and let a network add the higher orders. The path tracer already separates orders 0–1 from 2+, so the training target exists. Needs our own training.
  3. Network inside the march. A per-sample MLP fed by our light grid. WebVDB already has a small one. Fits procedural cloudscapes better, since it needs no box.

Our own path trace of the tower found the fit lighting 25–35 % too dark on lit faces. That gap is the kind of error these networks remove.

Protections

These exist because earlier work got stuck in gates, parity and long runs.

1One task = one change + one check. About 20 minutes of agent work, at most 5 minutes of rendering. Hand-back: done / stuck on / proposed next.
2The check is a picture. A side-by-side sheet judged by eye. A difference nobody can see, or under about 15 % in brightness, is not a task.
3Two tries. If a step fails its check twice, stop: log it as open and move on, or ask you.
4Parity is not the goal. A difference from the paper that we understand is a row in DIVERGES.
5No timing or tuning before N7, apart from one measurement per phase.
6Fixed check set. The Disney cloud and one of ours, noon and low sun, 256² or 512².
7Nothing runs over 30 minutes without a stated decision that depends on it and your yes.
8Never remove code. New behaviour goes behind a setting; anything on the bench gets a Review switch.
9Stuck or drifting agents stop and say so. A fresh agent with a short brief replaces one that overran.
10Out of scope until you say otherwise: cloudscapes, terrain or lights inside the cloud, phones, a temporal model, matching the paper's PSNR.
