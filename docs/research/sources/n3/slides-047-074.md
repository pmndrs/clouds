# Nubis Cubed, slides 47–74: voxel prototype, Burning Shores brief, voxel cloud modelling
Source: Schneider, *Nubis Cubed*, SIGGRAPH 2023 Advances (`artifacts/research/nubis/Nubis Cubed - 4k.pptx`, id `n3`).
Slide numbers are pptx presentation order. PDF-with-notes pages for this range are p055–p082 (PDF page = slide + 8).
Tags: authoring shape perf · Range status: 🟦 note written, 🟩 code (none in range).

## In one paragraph
This stretch has no shader code. It is the bridge from the 2022 multi-box voxel prototype to the shipped Burning Shores
system, and the first half of "Voxel Clouds / Modeling". What it teaches: (1) clouds were **grown with a fluid sim**
(the 2014 Aero solver, a custom Houdini microsolver network) and **composited** in an in-house Houdini toolset called
**Atlas** ("frankenclouding"); (2) the composite is baked into **one dense world grid**, not many boxes; (3) that grid is
**very coarse** (8 m voxels, 512×512×64 for 4 km × 4 km × ~500 m) because memory and bandwidth rule out anything finer,
and **all surface detail comes from the runtime up-rez** (slides 75+). Slide 66 shows this best: a voxelized game mesh (a
Tallneck) renders as a believable billowy cloud. The authored field only needs the silhouette and the gradients; the
billows come from the runtime noise.

## Slide-by-slide

**s47 — prototype step placement.** Diagram: a camera ray first jumps to the bounding-box entry (red circle), then an SDF
jump to the cloud surface (green arrow), then regular samples inside (red dots). Notes: the prototype combined bounding
boxes with *internal* signed distance fields into a "source-agnostic" step-placement method. It cites Nubis Evolved 2022
slides 192–205. No numbers. Figure: useful only as a march diagram.

**s48 — overlapping volumes.** That method supported overlapping volumes for sample placement, density and light. Figure
`image76`: one prototype voxel cumulus at sunset, a compact cauliflower with a flat underside. Good isolated shape ref.

**s49 — sample debug view.** `image77`: a RenderBuffer XRay of `Clouds - Oro LowRes - Attr` (RG_FLOAT_16, 960×540, G
channel) shows where samples were taken. The box volume shows as stacked slabs and the cloud interior is resolved inside
it. The Scalpel GPU profiler is visible. Notes: he iterated alone until he couldn't make it any faster. Reading which
brightness means what is my inference; the slide doesn't label it.

**s50 — prototype cost: about 4 ms at 960×540 on PS5** for "a few" clouds, with fly-in. Four renders (`image78–81`):
backlit stratocumulus, a chain of cumulus, and two cumulus towers rising from a cloud deck. Then the art director asked
for this in the DLC trailer.

**s51 — trailer animatic** (video `media10.mp4`, not extracted). The clouds were laid out as **white ellipsoid proxies**.
The shot needs fast flight through billowy clouds from foreground to far background. Still `image82` (shot sh020): a
low-poly icosphere proxy in front of placeholder clouds. Layout lesson: art direction blocked out cloudscapes with
ellipsoids. Our `build_heroes.py` sphere sources are the same idea (see below).

**s52 — prototype stress test failed:** "performance was terrible" and the detail wasn't there. `image83` (reused on s67)
shows big cumulus with **visible box faces / seams** at the bottom, which is the multi-box problem. The rescue: Burning
Shores was PS5-only, which removed many performance limits.

**s53–55 — the plan was "all or nothing":** whatever the trailer showed had to ship in-game at the same quality, or the
feature would be cut. Credits: Krebbers, Vos, McLaren, Malan. s55 is the trailer video (`media11.mp4`; the still is only
the PEGI card).

**s56–57 — takeaways.** `image86` and `image87` are trailer frames with dense, billowy cumulus layered in depth,
including a mountain top poking through. The art director kept the 200 m peak visible in a shot set about 1 km up. Clouds
became a foreground depth tool. Viewers took the trailer for a pre-rendered cinematic. Good figures for layering in depth.

**s58 — DLC requirements** (concept art, Viz Team). The clouds are a volumetric skybox *and* can be flown into from the
ground; the player can then dive into the water, all **without seams**. Teaser: "something even more challenging" comes
later (the storm/boss, other range).

**s59 — title, "Voxel Clouds, Est. 2023".** `image92` is the **best isolated hero shape in the range**: a voxel cumulus on
black, a broad flat-based body with a taller turret on one side and a ragged, thin skirt along the base.

**s60 — recap of the march loop:** density, then direct light (a second march), ambient, secondary (lightning), with
uninformed steps until opaque. The voxel approach replaces "almost every one" of these. Covered in other ranges.

**s61 — opening of the modelling section.** The point: modelling choices and their memory decide render performance.
`image93`: in-game sunset cumulus and congestus over the LA ruins, seen while flying (a shape reference with layered
depth).

**s62 — density sampler split.** The 2.5D sampler had five steps: (1) sample modelling data, (2) build the dimensional
profile, (3) build the noise composite, (4) up-rez the profile with noise, (5) post-process. With voxels, the
construction work moves **out of the shader** into a *Voxel Cloud Modeler* plus *Voxel Cloud Storage*. That leaves three
problems: model, store, and sample efficiently. (s69 repeats this with the sampler reduced to "1. Sample Voxel Cloud";
s75+ adds the up-rez back.)

**s63 — modelling methods considered:** metaballs, voxelized meshes, particles, fluid simulation. Schneider: the
hand-modelling routes are "tedious and unrealistic". The approach that worked best for him in the past was to
**"grow" clouds with fluid simulation**. This matches his Rio (2011) workflow [s3] and the 2014 Aero work [s6–8].

**s64 — Atlas / Aero Solver, Houdini.** They un-shelved the 2014 fluid-sim experiments ("I'm a data hoarder") and
frankenclouded them into Burning Shores cloudscapes. The video is `media3.mp4`, the **same clip as slide 7** (2014 Aero
sims). I extracted frames to scratch only: four Houdini perspective views. At frame 0 each sim is a **thin, flat,
horizontal lens or slab of density at the future cloud base**. By about 1 s it is a small humilis with a flat base; by
about 4 s it is a mediocris or congestus. The base stays flat at the seed height and stays roughly as wide as the
seed. Growth goes upward into cauliflower turrets, with some lateral spread at the base. Whether the seed is an initial
condition or a continuous emitter is not stated (my reading of the frames). The Aero solver graph (s6, another range;
one frame) lists *Apply Buoyant Force, Advect Density, Advect Velocity, Non-Divergence, Sourcing → Solve*, built from
Houdini **microsolvers**. s7 says it was "streamlined… to simulate only cloud growth", unlike combustion-style solvers.
**No solver parameters appear anywhere in the deck.**

**s65 — the Atlas toolset (Houdini).** "At its heart… a compositing pipeline for voxel data." Feature list, verbatim
structure:
- Voxel compositing tools
- Cloud simulation
- Sourcing: voxels, point clouds, meshes, and 2.5D authoring data
- Editing / manipulation: **cutouts, erosion, squashing**

No node names, HDA names or parameters are given. "Squashing" isn't defined further; I read it as non-uniform vertical
scaling of a sourced cloud, which is an inference.

**s66 — Tallneck example** (artist Bryan Adams). `image94` is the Houdini viewport. The Tallneck **game mesh** stands
among other sources. Left and right are high-detail sim clouds (white). In the middle are smooth, dark-grey lobed shapes
that look like a different source type; which one isn't stated. Everything sits on a terrain proxy. `image95` is the
in-game result: the mesh reads as a towering cloud with a mushroom cap and a ribbed neck, and its surface is fully
billowy. **Key lesson for us:** a plain voxelized mesh became a convincing cloud, so the billows come from the runtime
up-rez, not from the authored voxels. This is the strongest argument in the range that our authoring should get the
8 m silhouette, profile gradient, detail type and density scale right and leave surface detail to the shader.

**s67 — multiple boxes "bounded us both figuratively and literally"**: they had to stay resident, and switching
between them in the ray-march inner loop was slow (`image83`, with box faces visible).

**s68 — so: one dense grid.** After the whole cloudscape is composited, write **one dense voxel grid** holding cloud
density for every cubic metre of the world. The diagram reads "Density Field 4096 × 4096 × 512 voxels" (at 1 m).

**s70 — "memory bottlenecks"** (a cartoon of clouds in a bottle). **s71 — budget:** the DLC map is about **4 km × 4 km**
and needs about **500 m** of vertical cloud space, usable from the ground and in the air, with enough resolution for
flying through.

**s72 — lowered target: 2048 × 2048 × 256 at 2 m.** Flying mounts have a wingspan of about 2 m, so 2 m was judged
"barely enough".

**s73 — resolution vs memory table** (single-channel; slide values are decimal units, truncated):

| Grid (voxels) | Voxel | Uncompressed, 2 B/texel | BC4, 0.5 B/texel | Sparse? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4096×4096×512 | 1 m | 17.178 GB | 4.294 GB | ? |
| 2048×2048×256 | 2 m | 2.146 GB | 536.870 MB | ? |
| 1024×1024×128 | 4 m | 268.434 MB | 67.108 MB | ? |
| 512×512×64 | 8 m | 33.554 MB | 8.388 MB | ? |

I checked the arithmetic (it is exact before truncation). The voxel sizes are derived from 4 km / dims; the slide
doesn't print them. The notes give the reasons:
- Dense grids cost memory, and bandwidth per ray-march sample grows with them; early tests showed fine grids "simply not
  going to work".
- Sparse formats need an **indirection step per sample**, which raises the cost of every sample in an intensive march.
  With an "insanely small" schedule they didn't take on that unknown; it is flagged as future work.
- Decision: **"start with BC4 compressed, 8 meter precision"**, i.e. 512×512×64 (8 m × 64 = 512 m covers the 500 m
  need).
- Later in the deck (s80, other range) the shipped modelling NVDF is 512×512×64 in **BC6, 1 B/texel, 3 channels**
  (profile, detail type, density scale), 16.777 MB. BC4 was the single-channel starting point.

**s74 — summary of voxel modelling:** (1) grow clouds with fluid sims (the custom **Aero** solver in Houdini); (2) edit
and composite them into "Frankencloudscapes"; (3) store them in "obscenely low resolution" voxel grids sampled at render
time. The next section (s75+, up-rez) explains why that is enough.

Trivial or decorative: s53, s55 (video), s60 (recap), s69 (repeat of s62), s70 (cartoon).

## Gaps and try-next (this range)
1. **Test the s66 claim on our runtime.** Voxelize a plain recognizable mesh at 8 m, build profile, detail type and
   density scale from its SDF, render with our up-rez, and compare to a Cloud SOP hero. If the mesh reads as a cloud,
   drop the baked fine noise passes and author only silhouette and fields.
2. **Seed sims like Aero.** Use a thin flat lens or slab of density/heat at the cloud base (a one-shot initial condition
   or a short pulse) instead of a continuous hot-spot emitter, with working buoyancy. Compare against the s64 frames
   (flat base at seed height, base width ≈ seed width).
3. **Composite-time squash and erode** in `stampCloud`: non-uniform (x, y, z) scale and an erosion amount, matching
   Atlas's edit list [s65].
4. **BC-compressed NVDF tier** where `texture-compression-bc` and `texture-compression-bc-sliced-3d` exist. This cuts
   the modelling field 4× [s73, s80].
5. Keep the dense 8 m world grid as the baseline. Gate the sparse fine overlay on a measured per-sample cost [s73].
